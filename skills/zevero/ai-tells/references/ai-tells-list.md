# AI tells

Words, phrases and constructions that signal AI authorship. Avoid them in anything intended to read as human.

**How to use this list:** run a literal scan against the list itself, with the file open, rather than from memory. A recall-based scan only catches the tells that happen to be salient, and it grows. The de-tell rewrite is the most dangerous moment in the process: synonym-replacement draws from exactly this banned vocabulary, so a from-memory pass can introduce a new tell while removing others. Check every replacement phrase against the list before writing it.

Two things the scan usually misses:

1. **File content, not just the covering message.** A document written into a file and about to be called finished is a draft being presented. Scan the document, not the note attached to it.
2. **Inherited and reused text.** These words slip through most often when a draft is built by editing an earlier draft, because reused lines feel already-approved and skip fresh review. They are not exempt. Spoken-register provenance is not an exemption either: a phrase can work aloud and still read as an AI tell on the page.

**Treat the impulse as the tell.** The urge to add "genuinely / really / truly" to prop up a claim is itself the signal that the claim needs a concrete reason or should be stated plainly. Supply the reason or delete the intensifier, rather than softening it.

---

## Punctuation

- **Em dashes (—).** Strongly associated with AI writing. Use commas, brackets, colons, or restructure the sentence. Scan output literally for the "—" character before presenting any draft.

## Filler affirmations

- Genuinely
- Honestly
- Genuine (as in "genuine fit", "genuine opportunity")

## Faux-casual openers

- "Quick one —"

## Performative availability

- "Happy to jump on a call"
- **"No pressure"** as a closer or softener ("no pressure on those", "no pressure at all").
- **"No problem at all if not" / "No worries at all if not".** A decline-softener tacked onto an ask ("Would you be happy for me to introduce you? No problem at all if not!"). Reads as AI over-reassurance. Offering an easy out is fine, but say it plainly ("Let me know either way") or just ask the question and trust them to decline.

## Performed emotion

- "That one mattered"
- "Sit with that"
- **Things "landing"** ("that really landed"), including the transitive **"makes X land" / "what makes it land"** ("that background is what makes every chapter land", "the detail is what makes it land"). Same performed-impact problem in an active-voice disguise. Say what the thing actually does.
- **"Lands harder" / "lands harder than".** The comparative form, used to assert one phrasing beats another ("X lands harder than any lecture"). Performed impact instead of showing it. Say what the thing does, or just say it.
- **Things "mattering"** used as an intransitive closer ("this matters", "that matters" as a paragraph-ender).
- **"Really matters".** The emphatic version ("this really matters", "what really matters here"). Same problem, dialled up. Say the concrete thing that is important and why, or drop it.
- **"matters to us" / "X matters to me/us" / "we care about/deeply about X".** The mattering tell pointed at the writer or their organisation: attaching vague conviction to yourself instead of naming what you actually do about the thing. An empty statement of caring. Replace with the concrete action and the caring is shown rather than claimed. Example fix: "The SEND angle matters to us too" becomes "The SEND angle is something we are actively working on improving coverage for too".

## Unnamed transformation ("something shifts")

- **"Something shifts" / "something changes" / "something happens"** as a standalone dramatic fragment, usually after a "from the moment..." set-up ("From the moment guests walk through the door at Devon's Cookery School. Something shifts."). Two tells stacked. First, the vague noun: the writer promises a change and then refuses to name it, so the reader is asked to supply the feeling the sentence has not earned. Second, the deliberate sentence fragment used as a drum beat, which is the commonest AI move for manufacturing atmosphere. Name what actually changes ("the noise drops and everyone starts chopping"), in one full sentence. This also covers the whole **"From the moment you [do X]..."** opener, which nearly always arrives towing one of these.

## "The kind of X that makes you Y"

- **"the kind of background that makes you pay attention"**, and every variant of *the kind of [noun] that makes you [react]*: "the kind of CV that makes you sit up", "the kind of story that stays with you", "the kind of detail that makes you rethink everything". The construction tells the reader what to feel instead of giving them the fact that would produce the feeling, and it hides the fact completely: nothing in "a background that makes you pay attention" says what the background IS. Close sibling of the "landing" family. Replace with the specific credential or fact and stop. Also flagged in itself: **"makes you pay attention"** in any wrapper.

## Stock specifics and filler intensifiers

- **"Tuesday"** as the randomly-chosen ordinary day ("what would a Tuesday feel like", "a typical Tuesday", "on a Tuesday morning"). AI reaches for Tuesday by default as the generic everyday. If you want a concrete day, choose a real, specific one tied to the point, or drop the device. Also the **"X is just Tuesday"** construction, using Tuesday to mean unremarkable or routine ("In most of Europe, putting your toddler in childcare is just Tuesday", "for them it's just Tuesday"). Same default reflex in idiom form. Say it is ordinary or routine in plain words, or name what actually makes it unremarkable.
- **"In real time".** Filler intensifier that almost always adds nothing ("happening in real time", "watching it in real time"). Cut it, or say what actually happens.

## Atmospheric adjectives

- **"Quiet" / "quietly"** as a mood word ("a quiet place to think out loud", "a quiet look back", "quiet dots"), including the adverb ("quietly resurfaced", "quietly building", "things quietly changed"). Reads as AI reaching for calm-sounding ambience instead of saying something concrete. Use a specific description or drop it.
- **"Gently" / "follow up gently" / "gentle nudge".** A softener on an action that performs politeness instead of doing the thing. Drop it: "wanted to follow up" alone is warmer and cleaner.

## AI "moments" framing

- **"Moments".** Describing things as "moments that feel like you", or helping people "find moments" in ordinary life ("Leah helps you find moments that feel like you — even in the chaos of summer"). AI atmospheric abstraction. Name the specific thing or say what actually happens.

## Pressure / wellness language

- **"With purpose, not pressure"**, and similar guilt-removal framings ("unplug with purpose, not pressure", "Banish the screen-time guilt and judgement to help you unplug with purpose, not pressure"). Corporate-wellness phrasing that reads as AI-crafted softening. Say what you actually mean.

## Unearned insider claims

- **"Haven't noticed yet" / "probably doing it wrong" / "most leaders skip".** Claiming other people have not noticed something, are probably getting it wrong, or are skipping something important, without any justification, especially in headlines and hooks. Examples: "Most leaders haven't noticed yet." / "The shadow AI signal your governance framework is probably misreading." / "3 Things Elite Coaches Do That Most Leaders Skip." Asserts insider advantage or hidden knowledge to manufacture scarcity. Say what is actually overlooked and why, or drop the device.
- **Any "no one knows this / no one thinks about this / no one notices / no one realises" framing**, claiming that nobody except the writer sees or knows the thing. Drop the claim of universal ignorance; state the point.
- **"Here's the part most [X] miss" / "the part most business owners miss".** The same manufactured-insider move in its commonest sales-email form. State the point directly.

## Self-labelling tone or quality

- **Labelling the content's own tone or quality** ("a calm, clear guide", "a simple, practical framework", "this clear and honest piece", "your calm space to..."). Telling the reader the thing is calm, clear, simple or honest is self-congratulation and reads as AI. If it IS clear, the reader will feel it. Describe what the content IS by its format ("three things to do", "a step-by-step guide"), never by the quality you hope it has. Applies to body text, titles, subheadings, section headers and email copy alike.
- **"Put concretely" / "put simply" / "to be concrete" / "concretely,".** A signpost promising the reader you are about to be admirably clear. Self-congratulatory, same family as labelling the content's tone. Just say the concrete thing; the concreteness speaks for itself. Applies especially to section headings ("The risk, put concretely"). Also watch **"my honest read" / "the honest version"** as sibling self-praise.
- **"Answered properly" / "a proper answer" / "done properly".** Grading your own answer in a heading or intro ("Your Google Drive question, answered properly"). Whether the answer was proper is the reader's verdict to give, and claiming it in advance both spends credibility and invites the reader to look for the gap. Name the topic; let the answer earn the adjective.
- **"The honest limitations" / "honest limitations" / "to be honest about...".** The heading-form sibling of "my honest read". Worse than ordinary self-praise because it is trust-erosive by implicature: marking one section as honest tells the reader that honesty is the exception, so everything unmarked is now suspect. Candour is the unmarked default. Sections get named by their content ("What doesn't work yet"), never by the virtue they claim.
- **Announcing your own line's rhetorical force.** "That gives us a powerful line:", "the killer stat is", "our strongest argument here is", used to introduce your own forthcoming point. If the line is powerful the reader will feel it; the announcement only weakens it. State the fact on its own.

## Clichés and stock phrases

- **"Hiding in plain sight".** Overused metaphor that reads as AI filler. Say what is actually there and why it was overlooked, or drop it.
- **"...is the entire game"**, and variants ("that's the whole game", "the entire ballgame"). Stock emphasis that asserts importance instead of showing it. Example: "That distinction, instance versus class, is the entire game."
- **"Wearing" as a metaphor, in any use** ("came back wearing a slightly different hat", "a symptom wearing a root cause's name badge", "one principle wearing three outfits", "wearing a disguise"). AI reaches for the costume image to describe "similar but not identical". Describe the actual resemblance and difference. **"Dressed up as"** ("a demand dressed up as an incentive") and **"in disguise"** ("anxiety in disguise") are the same costume family, banned in any metaphorical use.
- **"Heavy lifting"**, and variants ("doing most of the heavy lifting", "does the heavy lifting"). Tired metaphor that asserts effort or importance instead of naming it. Say what actually does the work.
- **Something "lives in" something else** ("it all lives in the detail", "the answer lives in the implementation", "the risk lives in the gap"). AI reaches for "lives in" to locate an abstraction somewhere. Say where the thing actually is, or state it plainly ("the difference is in the detail").

## Comparative swagger

- **Advertising-formula comparatives.** "fixes a gap X doesn't touch", "solves what X can't", "does what X only promises": any variant of "removes stains other products can't" or "refreshes the parts other beers cannot reach". This is the skeleton of competitive ad copy, and in prose it reads as AI reaching for punch. State the fact plainly and let the comparison sit where it already is.
- **Redundant comparative jab.** Re-asserting a comparison inside a sentence when a nearby heading, label or column has already made it (a "Further than X" cell whose sentence repeats "which X doesn't do"). Zero new information, so all that is left is attitude, and performed superiority with no content is the common core of most tells on this list. Delete the jab; keep the fact.

## Faux-reflective signposting

- **"so they're worth knowing" / "worth knowing".** Telling the reader the content is valuable instead of delivering it. A self-justifying signpost. Cut it; if the content earns its place the reader sees that for themselves. Example: "there are only three ways an app can work out a child's age... so they're worth knowing".
- **"Which is the part I keep coming back to" / "the bit I keep thinking about".** Manufactured reflection that pretends at a personal thinking process to dress up a transition. Make the point directly.

## Vague evaluative filler

- **"something real" / "buys us something real" / "a real step".** Gestures at significance without naming it. "Real" as a vague intensifier on an abstract noun is AI hedging. Say what the concrete thing is and what it does.

## Abstraction-as-protagonist epigrams

Sentences whose subject is an abstract noun (compliance, enforcement, ambition, the design, the market) doing something metaphorical (arrive, carry, surface, hold, defend). No human actor, no picturable action, nothing checkable: concepts acting on concepts. Usually deployed as a paragraph-closing maxim, often bolted on with "which is why...", or as mirrored headings and clause-pairs whose matched rhythm is decorative. The sentence performs conclusiveness instead of adding information, because the sentence before it already did the work.

- **"...which is why enforcement cannot carry the design".** Two abstractions joined by a metaphorical verb, appended as a summarising epigram. The human version names actors and literal actions: "you cannot inspect private homes, so the rules go unenforced".
- **"Where compliance was expected, it did not arrive"** (as a heading). Personified abstraction, compliance arriving like a train, plus an actor-deleting passive ("was expected", by whom?), plus construction as one of a matched pair with identical rhythm ("Where compliance was made worth it, the market surfaced"). Headings written by people state the topic or claim in plain word order ("What happens without an incentive").
- Siblings: **"Where informality is normal, both sides of the market defend it"** (an epigram closer restating the statistic before it: cut it, the statistic carries). **"the difference is not the strictness of the rules, it is which side of the ledger the state put its money on"** (antithesis plus ledger metaphor: say "France pays families to declare").
- **The test:** can you picture the subject doing the verb? If not, give the sentence a concrete subject (families, the state, the tax authority) and a literal verb, or delete it, because an epigram at the end of a paragraph is usually a restatement.
- **Staged-transition variant:** **"Germany answers it" / "France runs the opposite design"**, a country, case or object personified as making a rhetorical move, used as a paragraph opener. Same family, worse consequence: it stages the point without stating it, so the reader cannot scan the paragraph and learn what the case shows. The opposite design of what? Answering what? Fix by opening the paragraph with the conclusion in plain words: "Germany shows the same outcome in a famously rule-following culture"; "France shows that when the state pays families to declare, they declare."

## Faux-insight and coaching closers

A cluster of short, knowing appraisal-phrases that perform insight or hand out coaching instead of saying the thing. Avoid all:

- **"That's the spine" / "the spine".** Declaring the core or structure of something.
- **"Fair hit".** Faux-concession acknowledging a point.
- **"That's the whole thing".** Variant of "...is the entire game".
- **"Sharpen that" / "say the word".** Coaching imperatives dressed as encouragement.
- **"Notice the arc" / "the arc of what just happened".** Narrating a supposed shape onto events.
- **"One honest caveat" / "honest caveat".** Sibling to "my honest read"; announcing candour instead of being candid.
- **"stated plainly" / "the full amount, stated plainly".** Sibling to "put concretely"; promising plainness. Just say the thing.
- **"That's doing real work" / "doing real work".** Asserting that a phrase or move is effective, instead of showing it. Sibling to "something real" and "heavy lifting".
